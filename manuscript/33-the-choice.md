# Chapter 33: The Choice

## Chapter purpose
Harcourt explains the options. Adrian can bury, settle quietly, or disclose. He chooses full disclosure.

## Mystery movement
- **Clues:** C12/C18/C19 implications
- **Red herrings:** None
- Evidence must be observed before it is interpreted.
- POV inference cannot exceed the physical/documentary record.

## Romance / relationship movement
His apology becomes action rather than persuasion.

## End hook
Talia signs a statement authenticating the cassette.

## Draft

Peter Harcourt brought two barristers, three folders and no optimism.

Claire considered this appropriate.

The meeting took place in the dining room because the library had become evidence storage and nobody wanted coffee near Sebastian’s confession.

Claire’s independent solicitor joined by video from London.

That mattered.

She sat on one side of the table as archivist and potential Mercer descendant, not as Adrian’s anything.

Adrian sat opposite Harcourt.

Talia and Sophie took the far end.

Felix was absent by agreement and legal advice.

Harcourt began with the simplest fact.

“The proposed sale is suspended.”

Adrian nodded.

“The title issue is now material. The 1932 deed is almost certainly invalid if the execution date and death record are correct, and the preliminary handwriting opinion is adverse.”

Claire’s solicitor added, “Any historical beneficial or proprietary claim by Eleanor’s descendants will depend on limitation, registration history, succession law and several other questions. Nobody should assume the house simply passes to Claire.”

“Good,” Claire said.

One barrister looked faintly surprised.

She ignored him.

The house was not a prize at the end of a mystery.

If the law produced a claim, it would be determined properly.

Harcourt opened the second folder.

“There are, broadly, three courses.”

“Here we go,” Sophie murmured.

“First, litigation. Every issue tested. Title, fraud, succession, limitation, equitable remedies. Expensive, public and potentially years long.”

“Second?” Adrian asked.

“Confidential negotiated settlement among relevant parties, with limited public statement regarding historical attribution.”

Claire heard what sat beneath the phrase.

Settle the money.

Reduce the history.

“And third?”

“Full disclosure of the archive findings, withdrawal of the sale, independent title review, and an attempt to build a settlement around preservation rather than immediate disposal.”

Sophie said, “That sounds suspiciously like the moral option.”

Harcourt looked pained.

“I am a solicitor. I do not rank options by morality at the table.”

Talia said, “But you know which one Marcus would choose.”

Harcourt did not answer.

Adrian looked at Claire.

Not for permission.

She watched carefully to see whether he would make the choice conditional on her reaction.

He did not.

“How quickly can we prepare disclosure?” he asked.

Harcourt’s face tightened.

“You are choosing the third route.”

“Yes.”

“You understand that publication of the forged conveyance and Sebastian’s letter may encourage claims beyond Claire’s. Lenders, insurers, heritage bodies, tax authorities—”

“Yes.”

“Press interest will be substantial.”

“Yes.”

“The Raven name will not survive this intact.”

Adrian sat back.

“That may be the first thing in this process that does not concern me.”

Claire felt something inside her shift.

Not forgiveness.

Evidence.

This was what change looked like when it stopped asking to be believed and became expensive.

Harcourt continued.

“Then we need a controlled preservation protocol, joint expert review and an immediate notice to the proposed buyer.”

“Do it.”

The buyer was informed that afternoon.

The marketing process stopped.

An independent archive was contacted about temporary custody of the most sensitive documents.

Claire’s solicitor began constructing a separate file for the Mercer line.

Adrian signed every instruction without asking Claire what outcome she wanted.

That restraint mattered too.

After the lawyers left, Raven House felt strangely quiet.

Sophie went to photograph the west stair in daylight for the preservation record.

Talia remained at the dining table.

Adrian stood by the window.

Claire gathered her notes.

“Claire.”

She looked up.

Adrian did not come closer.

“I owe you something that is not another apology.”

She waited.

“When I hired you, I thought I could keep uncertain information away from you until it became relevant. I told myself that was neutral.”

“Yes.”

“It wasn’t.”

“No.”

“I cannot undo that. I also cannot ask you to judge me by what I say now.”

Claire’s throat tightened.

“Then don’t.”

“I’m not.”

He looked towards the folders Harcourt had left.

“I’m going to do the thing whether you forgive me or not.”

That was the first sentence since the rupture that made reconciliation possible.

Not because it was romantic.

Because it removed Claire from responsibility for his integrity.

She nodded.

“That is what you should do.”

“I know.”

This time the phrase did not irritate her.

Talia stood.

“There is one more piece.”

She held the cassette case.

“What?” Adrian asked.

“My authentication.”

Talia had already dictated a statement to Harcourt’s assistant. Now she placed it on the table.

It identified the tape, the date and location of recording, the voices, the cassette’s custody, and every transfer of possession she could remember.

At the bottom was a blank signature line.

Talia picked up a pen.

Her hand trembled.

“You don’t have to do this without your own solicitor,” Claire said.

“I have spoken to one.”

“Good.”

Talia looked at the page.

“For years I told myself I was protecting Evelyn’s memory because she had already suffered. Then I told myself I was protecting Marcus. Then Adrian and Sophie. There was always another living person available to justify silence.”

Claire said nothing.

Talia signed.

**Talia Evelyn Raven.**

She dated it.

Then she pushed the statement towards Claire.

“There.”

Adrian looked at his aunt.

Talia met his eyes.

“Do better with what you inherit.”

He nodded.

The statement joined the evidence file.

A confession no longer existed only as a cassette hidden in a biscuit tin and remembered by one frightened family member.

It had provenance.

A witness.

A chain.

The truth was becoming harder to bury than to disclose.

That evening, an email arrived from Harcourt.

**Buyer acknowledges withdrawal. No further marketing activity. Independent title counsel instructed. Preservation notice issued.**

Claire read it twice.

Raven House was no longer for sale.

Not because Adrian had won it.

Not because Claire had claimed it.

Because, for the first time in almost a century, nobody responsible for it was pretending the title story was simple.
