# Chapter 22: The Dead Woman’s Signature

## Chapter purpose
Harcourt produces the 1932 transfer deed; Claire spots Celeste’s signature dated after her death.

## Mystery movement
- **Clues:** C12
- **Red herrings:** R3 resolves
- Evidence must be observed before it is interpreted.
- POV inference cannot exceed the physical/documentary record.

## Romance / relationship movement
Adrian refuses the easy option of stopping the inquiry.

## End hook
The sale cannot proceed cleanly.

## Draft

Peter Harcourt arrived carrying the past in a black document case and looking as though he blamed Claire for its weight.

“I would like it recorded,” he said before sitting, “that I advised against broadening the scope of this exercise.”

Claire opened her notebook.

“Recorded.”

“That was not an invitation to write it down.”

“It is now.”

Adrian closed the library door.

“Peter.”

Harcourt exhaled through his nose and removed a folder.

“The estate title bundle contains a 1932 conveyance by which Raven House passed from Celeste Vane to Sebastian Raven.”

Claire did not reach for it.

“Original?”

“Original deed retained with later abstract copies.”

“Provenance?”

“Continuous in the firm’s predecessor archive from at least 1951. Earlier custody is less clean.”

“That matters.”

“I am aware.”

Harcourt put the deed on the table.

The paper was heavier than the archive letters. Legal foolscap. Red revenue marks. Wax residue where a seal had once been attached.

Claire read the date first.

12 March 1932.

Then the grantor.

**Celeste Miriam Vane.**

Her skin prickled.

Celeste had died on 17 October 1931.

Claire checked again because impossibility should always be forced to repeat itself.

March 1932.

Five months after death.

She looked at Harcourt.

“When did you first notice?”

His mouth tightened.

“I noticed the date discrepancy during a preliminary title review after Marcus died.”

“And?” Adrian asked.

“I assumed either the family death date was wrong or the deed recited an earlier transaction imperfectly.”

“Did you verify?”

“I ordered historical searches. They were inconclusive.”

Claire pointed to the execution block.

There was Celeste’s signature.

Or an attempt at it.

At a glance it resembled the signature on the marriage certificate. Same sweeping C. Same narrow V.

At a second glance, it did not.

The terminal stroke of Vane was too short. The pressure pattern looked hesitant. Several letters had been drawn rather than written.

Claire photographed it beside a verified sample.

“Do not call this forged yet,” she said.

Harcourt blinked. “I had no intention of doing so.”

“I was speaking to Adrian.”

“I hadn’t said anything.”

“You were about to.”

He gave her a look.

Claire continued.

“We have a document dated after Celeste’s death bearing a signature purporting to be hers. Possibilities include erroneous dating, later execution under some authority we have not identified, a misrecorded death, or fraud.”

Harcourt said, “Thank you.”

“That was not for your comfort.”

“No. But I will take it.”

Adrian leaned over the deed.

“What does the conveyance actually do?”

“Transfers fee simple ownership of Raven House and the surrounding estate to Sebastian for nominal consideration,” Harcourt said.

“Nominal?”

“Ten pounds and other consideration.”

Claire looked up.

“Was Celeste’s estate probated?”

“Minimal probate record. Personal effects only. Raven House does not appear in the schedule.”

“Because this deed made it look as if she had disposed of it.”

“Yes.”

“And the deed is dated after she died.”

“Yes.”

Harcourt’s reluctance had vanished. Not because he was suddenly candid, Claire realised. Because the problem had become too large for euphemism.

Adrian said, “So you knew the title was questionable.”

“I knew it required investigation.”

“You also told Claire title was outside her remit.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because an archivist is not a conveyancing solicitor, and because premature speculation about defective title could prejudice a sale.”

Claire said, “That is not the same as hiding murder evidence.”

Harcourt looked at her with something like gratitude.

Adrian did not.

“He still tried to contain it.”

“He contained a legal issue he thought belonged to lawyers. Annoying. Possibly overcautious. Not evidence he knew why the deed was wrong.”

Harcourt sat back.

“I may frame that.”

Claire ignored him.

The red herring had collapsed into something more ordinary: professional risk aversion.

Less sinister.

No less consequential.

“Can the sale proceed?” Adrian asked.

Harcourt folded his hands.

“Not cleanly.”

“How uncleanly?”

“If a credible adverse claim exists, and if this deed is invalid, any buyer will require resolution or substantial protection. Once notified, we cannot simply pretend the issue does not exist.”

Adrian glanced at Claire.

“And if we stop investigating?”

Harcourt paused.

The question settled heavily.

Claire said nothing.

She needed Adrian’s answer to belong to him.

Harcourt chose his words carefully.

“You could limit further historical inquiry. You could seek counsel’s opinion on what is already known. You could negotiate warranties. There are ways to reduce uncertainty.”

“By not looking?” Adrian asked.

“I did not say that.”

“You almost did.”

Harcourt’s expression hardened.

“My job is to protect your legal position.”

Adrian looked at the impossible signature.

“My father’s note says not to let discretion become dishonesty.”

“Marcus was not practising law when he wrote that.”

“No. He was trying to leave me a conscience.”

Claire watched him.

This was the first real consequence.

Not an abstract possibility that the house had been stolen.

A sale that could fail.

Money that could disappear.

Family property that might no longer be family property.

Adrian looked at Harcourt.

“We keep going.”

Peter closed his eyes briefly.

“Of course you do.”

“We preserve everything, disclose what needs disclosing, and get independent counsel for Claire’s side if the Mercer connection becomes a claim.”

Claire felt something inside her ease.

Not because the choice benefited her.

Because he made it before asking what it would cost.

Harcourt gathered his papers except the deed, which remained for supervised examination.

“I will instruct a forensic document examiner,” he said. “And obtain counsel’s preliminary view.”

“Good,” Claire said.

At the door he paused.

“For what it is worth, Dr Mercer, I did not know Celeste was Sebastian’s wife.”

“I believe you.”

“You say that as though it disappoints you.”

“It makes the story less convenient.”

“Reality often does.”

He left.

Adrian remained by the table.

Claire looked at the two signatures side by side.

Marriage certificate, 1924.

Transfer deed, 1932.

One alive.

One dead.

“Thank you,” she said.

“For what?”

“Not stopping.”

He looked at her.

“I nearly did.”

She appreciated the admission more than certainty.

“When?”

“When Peter said we could contain it.”

“And?”

“I thought about the roofs, the staff, Felix’s expectation of money, Sophie wanting nothing to do with any of it, Talia getting older. Then I thought about a woman signing away her house five months after she was buried.”

Claire touched the edge of the document sleeve.

“That tends to focus the mind.”

His smile was brief.

The forensic examiner would later determine whether the signature was copied or simulated.

For now they had something simpler and stranger.

A dead woman had apparently transferred Raven House.

And because of that line of ink, the modern sale had stopped being a transaction.

It had become a question no buyer’s solicitor could responsibly ignore.
