# Chapter 3: Talia Raven

## Chapter purpose

## Mystery movement

## Draft

By breakfast, the voice in the wall had acquired three explanations and no owner.

Elspeth blamed the heating pipes.

Adrian blamed the geometry.

Claire blamed neither until someone proved one of them right.

She had slept badly, but not because she believed Raven House was haunted. That would have been easier. Ghosts required faith. Bad documentation required work.

At eight-fifteen she was in the workroom with the west-wing drawings spread beneath archival weights when Elspeth appeared carrying coffee on a silver tray.

The housekeeper placed the pot beside Claire without asking whether she wanted it.

“You’ll need that.”

“Why?”

“Miss Raven is coming down.”

Claire looked up. “Which Miss Raven?”

Elspeth’s face suggested there was only one who required warning.

“Miss Talia.”

“Adrian’s aunt.”

“You’ve read the family sheet.”

“I read everything I’m sent.”

Elspeth glanced at the plans. “That may not help.”

Then she left.

Claire poured coffee.

At nine precisely, Talia Raven entered without knocking.

She was tall, silver-haired and dressed in deep green wool that matched nothing in the room and somehow made everything else look temporary. Her face was not severe so much as exact. Claire had the immediate impression that Talia had spent a lifetime teaching other people to mistake precision for coldness because it was more useful that way.

Adrian came in behind her.

“Aunt Talia, Dr Claire Mercer.”

Talia’s gaze moved to Claire.

It stopped.

Not on her face.

On the name.

Claire had seen people recognise surnames before. Usually the reaction involved curiosity, an attempt to place a family, a question about where one was from.

This was different.

For perhaps half a second, Talia became perfectly still.

Then the expression vanished.

“Dr Mercer,” she said.

“Claire is fine.”

“I imagine it is.”

Adrian exhaled quietly through his nose.

Claire decided she liked Talia at once, which was unfortunate.

Talia walked to the table and inspected the plans without touching them.

“You have already opened Celeste’s drawings.”

“That is part of the commission.”

“So Adrian tells me.”

“Do you object?”

Talia looked at her then.

“I object to very little. I remember a great deal.”

There was something almost elegant about the warning.

Claire pulled out a chair. “Then you may be exactly who I need.”

Adrian looked away, possibly to hide amusement.

Talia did not sit.

“What have you found?”

“A discrepancy in the west-wing measurements. Original service structures omitted from the modern survey. Unidentified notation on Celeste’s plans.”

“Nothing more dramatic?”

“Should there be?”

Talia’s eyes flicked to Adrian.

“No.”

Claire registered the answer, not as evidence, but as pressure.

She showed Talia the paired-curve symbols.

“Do you recognise these?”

“No.”

Too quick.

“Have you seen them before?”

“No.”

Different question. Same answer.

Claire let the silence sit.

Talia gave no sign of discomfort.

“You worked in publishing, didn’t you?” Claire asked.

“For thirty-two years.”

“Then you know the difference between remembering a mark and knowing what it means.”

Talia’s mouth shifted by a degree.

“I also know the difference between an interview and an interrogation.”

“Good. I prefer interviews.”

“Do you?”

Adrian stepped in before the exchange became enjoyable.

“Talia knows more family history than anyone alive.”

“That is a rather low bar,” she said.

Claire turned a page in her notebook.

“Tell me about Celeste Vane.”

Talia finally sat.

For the next twenty minutes she gave Claire exactly the sort of account old families preserved when they wished to appear candid.

Celeste was brilliant.

Difficult.

Ahead of her time.

Close to Sebastian Raven during the design of the house.

Too independent to remain long under family patronage.

She left Raven House in the late 1920s after a disagreement over alterations.

Her death several years later was tragic but unrelated to the family.

Claire wrote without interruption.

“What year did she leave?”

“1928, I believe.”

“You believe?”

“Perhaps 1929.”

“And she did not return?”

“Not in any significant way.”

Claire looked up.

“What counts as significant?”

Talia’s gaze sharpened.

“You do enjoy verbs.”

“Nouns too, when properly documented.”

Adrian shifted in his chair.

Claire continued. “Was Celeste ever resident here?”

“For periods during construction.”

“After completion?”

“I couldn’t say.”

“Did she have a room?”

“All architects have rooms when a client is unwise enough to let them.”

Adrian said, “Talia.”

“What? It is true.”

Claire turned a page.

“Was there ever a child associated with Celeste?”

That did it.

Not much. A slight tightening at the base of Talia’s thumb where her hand rested on the table.

“No.”

Claire did not ask why she was certain.

Not yet.

Instead she said, “And Sebastian?”

“What about him?”

“Was he married when the house was built?”

“No.”

“Engaged?”

“Not to my knowledge.”

“When did Evelyn Ashcombe enter the picture?”

Talia’s posture changed so slightly it would have been easy to miss.

“Later.”

“How much later?”

“You have access to the family dates.”

“I have access to dates. I am asking about sequence.”

Talia leaned back.

“Dr Mercer, if your purpose is to catalogue Celeste Vane’s archive, catalogue it. If your purpose is to reconstruct every private attachment of dead people, you may discover that privacy becomes no less deserving merely because its owners can no longer object.”

Claire capped her pen.

“That is a respectable principle.”

“I’m glad you think so.”

“It becomes less respectable when privacy is used to distort authorship.”

Adrian looked between them.

Talia’s expression did not change.

“You think the family stole credit from Celeste.”

“I think history often does that for families before they need to do anything themselves.”

“And do you imagine yourself correcting history?”

“No.” Claire met her eyes. “I imagine myself correcting the catalogue.”

For the first time, Talia smiled.

It was not warm.

It was approval.

Which made Claire more suspicious, not less.

Talia rose.

“I shall leave you to your catalogue.”

At the door she paused.

“Adrian.”

He followed her into the corridor.

Claire did not listen.

She did, however, notice that their voices moved away from the room rather than staying outside it.

Privacy deserved respect even when other people did not.

She returned to the plans.

The west-wing drawing from 1925 showed three service chases. A later copy, probably after 1940, showed only one. Claire aligned the sheets under translucent polyester.

One wall line had shifted.

Then another.

At the end of the corridor, where the bricked doorway stood, the older plan suggested a narrow access recess.

She marked it for Jonah.

A different folder contained photographic contact sheets. Most were undated: masons, carpenters, scaffolding, Celeste in a dark coat standing beside stacks of stone. On one strip, she stood at the western elevation with a pencil behind her ear and a rolled drawing under one arm.

Claire enlarged the scan on her laptop.

Behind Celeste, the wall now occupied by later plastering was open.

Not a full room. A service aperture, perhaps.

Above it, chalked on the stone, someone had written a date.

The last two digits were blurred.

Claire zoomed until the image broke into grain.

Could be 25.

Could be 31.

She refused to decide.

The door opened.

Adrian returned alone.

“Did I survive?” Claire asked.

“Barely.”

“She dislikes me.”

“No.”

“You answered too quickly.”

“She dislikes uncertainty.”

“So do I.”

“That may be the problem.”

He took the chair Talia had vacated.

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Adrian said, “I’m sorry.”

Claire looked up.

“For what?”

“She came in here intending to establish territory.”

“She failed.”

“I noticed.”

“You do not need to apologise for your aunt.”

“I’m not.”

That made her pause.

“I’m apologising because I knew she would test you and I did not warn you properly.”

“That is different.”

“Yes.”

Claire studied him.

“You are very careful not to undermine her.”

“She raised Sophie and me through more of our adolescence than my parents did.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

“No.” He looked towards the door. “But it is the answer.”

There it was again: restraint that felt less like secrecy than discipline.

Claire understood discipline.

She disliked recognising it in him.

“She said Celeste left in 1928,” Claire said.

“That is what I was told growing up.”

“By whom?”

“Everyone.”

“Which means no one.”

He accepted that with a slight nod.

Claire turned the contact sheet towards him.

“This wall opening existed during construction. I can’t date the image accurately yet.”

Adrian leaned closer.

Their shoulders nearly touched.

Claire became aware of his aftershave, which irritated her because she did not want sensory detail from him becoming memorable.

He pointed at the chalk marks.

“That says thirty-one.”

“You cannot possibly know that.”

“I can read a three.”

“You can hope to read a three.”

He looked at her.

“You don’t allow yourself many assumptions, do you?”

“Assumptions are where other people hide.”

The sentence escaped before she could stop it.

Adrian’s expression changed.

Again, he did not ask.

Again, Claire noticed.

He stood.

“Jonah will be here in an hour.”

“Good.”

Adrian reached the door, then turned back.

“Talia asked me whether I checked your family background.”

Claire’s pen stopped.

“Why?”

“She didn’t say.”

“Did you?”

“Only professional references.”

That was normal.

Still, Claire thought of the half-second stillness when Talia heard her name.

“Mercer isn’t rare,” she said.

“No.”

“Did she say she knew one?”

“No.”

Claire wrote nothing.

Adrian left.

At lunch, Talia reappeared in the doorway while Claire was alone.

No preamble this time.

“Your mother’s family,” she said. “Mercer?”

“Yes.”

“Your grandmother used the name?”

“Yes.”

“Was Mercer her birth name?”

Claire frowned.

“I don’t know.”

Talia’s fingers tightened around the head of her walking stick, though Claire had not noticed her needing one earlier.

“She never told you?”

“She didn’t talk much about her life before my mother was born.”

That was true, and Claire disliked how suddenly the omission felt significant merely because Talia had asked about it.

“Did she use another surname?” Talia said.

“Not that I know of.”

Talia looked towards the rain-grey windows.

Claire waited.

When Talia spoke again, her voice was softer.

“Was your grandmother from the north?”

Claire studied her.

“I don’t know that either.”

Talia nodded once, as though the absence of an answer had confirmed something of its own.

Then she left.

Claire remained at the table with the uncomfortable sense that Talia Raven had asked three questions and revealed far more than she had answered.
