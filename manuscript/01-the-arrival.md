# Chapter 1: The Arrival

The house appeared only when the rain allowed it.

For the last mile, Claire Mercer had seen little beyond the windscreen: dry-stone walls slick with water, black hedges, the blurred silver of standing water on the road. Then the wipers cleared a clean arc, the land fell away to her left, and Raven House stood on the opposite rise as if it had been waiting for the weather to lift its hand.

Her first thought was that the photographs had lied.

Not about its beauty. Raven House was beautiful in the disciplined way some people were beautiful—because every unnecessary thing had been refused. Pale stone, long horizontal windows, a central block set between two lower wings. No turrets, no mock battlements, none of the theatrical medievalism its name suggested. The roofline stepped with the ground instead of fighting it. Even from the car she could see the intelligence in the proportions.

Her second thought was that the building was wider than it ought to be.

Claire leaned towards the glass.

“You all right back there?” the driver asked.

“Yes.”

“You’ve gone quiet.”

“I’m looking.”

He laughed as if that explained something about her. It usually did.

The approach curved through a stand of beech trees, their wet trunks shining almost white. Raven House disappeared behind them, reappeared closer, vanished once more. Claire counted bays without meaning to. Five across the central elevation. Three on the west wing. The east looked shorter from this angle, although the archive photographs suggested symmetry.

She opened the folder on her lap and checked the printed survey plan.

The driver glanced at it in the mirror. “You planning to buy it?”

“God, no.”

“Good. You look too sensible.”

That made her smile.

The car climbed the final bend and stopped in a gravel court where rain had collected in every shallow depression. Up close, the house looked less pale than the photographs: grey-buff stone darkened at the joints, window frames almost black, brass weathered green at the front doors. The central entrance was recessed beneath a flat stone canopy so thin it looked structurally improbable.

Someone had designed this, Claire thought, who trusted lines more than decoration.

Celeste Vane.

The name had been the reason Claire accepted the commission before she had quite decided whether taking it was sensible.

The driver unloaded her suitcase and archive case. Claire stood under the canopy and looked back at the west wing.

Three bays.

She looked at the plan in her hand.

Two principal rooms and a service corridor.

Possible, she told herself. Walls swallowed space. Old houses accumulated ducts, cupboards, blocked flues, badly drawn surveys and generations of contractors who treated plans as suggestions.

Still.

She folded the paper and put it away.

The door opened before she could knock.

The man behind it wore no jacket, only a dark shirt with the sleeves turned back once at the wrist. He was taller than she had expected from the photographs supplied by the estate office, and less polished. The image she had been sent showed a man in a suit beside a charity plaque. This one had a pencil tucked behind one ear and a faint streak of plaster dust on his forearm.

“Dr Mercer?”

“Claire is fine.”

A pause, almost too brief to count.

“Adrian Raven.”

“I recognised you.”

“I’m relieved. Otherwise this would be a very bad beginning.”

His voice was quieter than she expected. Dry rather than warm.

Claire handed him nothing. People often reached automatically for a woman’s bags when they wanted to establish usefulness. Adrian looked at the archive case, then at her.

“Which one contains irreplaceable paper?”

“The black one.”

He took the suitcase.

Better.

Inside, the entrance hall was warmer but not warmer-looking. The stone floor continued through without a rug. Oak panelling rose only to shoulder height, above it lime plaster the colour of old bone. No ancestral portraits crowded the walls. A single bronze light fitting hung in the central void.

Claire stopped beneath it.

The hall was smaller than the façade suggested.

Not small. Smaller.

Adrian had gone three steps before noticing she was no longer beside him.

“Problem?”

“Orientation.”

“You’ve been inside for twelve seconds.”

“Thirteen.”

He looked at his watch.

“That was a mistake,” she said.

Something moved at one corner of his mouth.

“Come through.”

He led her across the hall. Claire followed, letting herself register surfaces rather than impressions. Original oak, probably. Later electrical channels concealed badly near the skirting. Lime plaster repaired with gypsum in at least two places. Door furniture mostly period, except one lever handle in polished brass that offended the rest of the building by being aggressively new.

The corridor beyond the hall narrowed before turning west.

Claire glanced back.

From outside, the western elevation had looked generous enough to contain rooms with depth. Inside, the transition felt compressed. She marked the thought and did not yet trust it.

Adrian opened a door at the end of the corridor.

“This is where you’ll work.”

The room had once been a library or study. Built-in shelves covered two walls. A large table stood in the centre beneath three green-shaded lamps. On it waited acid-free boxes, pencils, nitrile gloves, paper weights and a laptop dock.

Claire set down her archive case.

“You bought archival weights?”

“I was given a list.”

“And you followed it.”

“Should I apologise?”

“Not yet.”

This time he did smile.

It changed his face less than she would have preferred.

She turned to the shelves.

Twelve grey document boxes occupied the lowest run. More sat on a trolley near the window. Each bore a temporary label in black marker: VANE / PLANS, VANE / LETTERS, HOUSE / GENERAL, HOUSE / ALTERATIONS.

“Who sorted these?”

“My father started. Then a local archivist made an inventory after he died.”

“Name?”

“Eleanor Price. Retired now.”

“No relation to Jonah Price?”

“You have done your homework.”

“That wasn’t an answer.”

“His aunt.”

Claire nodded and opened her notebook.

Adrian watched her write it down.

“That matters?”

“Everything matters until it doesn’t.”

He folded his arms. “That sounds exhausting.”

“It’s why you hired me.”

The silence between them was not hostile. It had edges, though.

He moved to the far side of the table.

“The brief is straightforward. The house is being prepared for sale. Before that happens, I want Celeste Vane’s surviving material catalogued properly. Drawings, correspondence, photographs, anything that can establish provenance or significance.”

“Conservation significance or market significance?”

“Both.”

“Those are not always the same thing.”

“I’m aware.”

“Are you?”

His eyes came up to hers.

There it was: the first clean point of friction.

Claire had met men who mistook possession for expertise, especially when the possession was old, expensive and inherited. She had also met clients who wanted a scholar’s authority without a scholar’s conclusions. Adrian Raven had not yet proved himself either kind.

But the room had been prepared exactly as requested.

She gave him one point back.

“What are the sale deadlines?”

“Six weeks before final documentation. Four before the marketing package is locked.”

“That’s not enough time for a complete archive.”

“I know.”

“You just said the brief was straightforward.”

“The objective is straightforward. The timing is not.”

“Better.”

His eyebrow lifted.

“You score answers?”

“Only privately.”

“I feel reassured.”

Claire looked again at the boxes. “And access?”

“Anywhere relevant to the archive.”

“That phrase will become a problem.”

“Why?”

“Because houses don’t organise evidence according to the convenience of their owners.”

“Former owners, potentially.”

“Current owner until someone signs something.”

“That sounds almost optimistic.”

She closed the notebook.

“I’ll need access to service rooms, roof spaces if the drawings require it, cellars, sealed cupboards, former bedrooms, estate offices. I may need to photograph construction details. If I find something embedded in the building, I stop before disturbing it and bring in the conservation surveyor.”

“Jonah.”

“Yes.”

“He’ll be here tomorrow.”

“Good.”

“I also need you to tell me before you open anything structural.”

“That is what I just said.”

“I heard you.”

“Then why repeat it?”

“Because this is my house.”

The words landed harder than his tone.

Claire looked at him.

Adrian seemed to hear them a second time himself.

He unfolded his arms.

“My responsibility,” he said. “That was the point.”

“Better again.”

“Do I get the point back?”

“No.”

For a moment she thought he might laugh. Instead he looked towards the rain-blurred windows.

“My father believed Celeste Vane mattered,” he said. “More than the family ever acknowledged. I’m trying to find out whether he was right.”

That, at least, sounded unpractised.

Claire rested one hand on the table.

“She mattered.”

“You say that before opening a box.”

“I say it because the building exists.”

He looked around the room as if the answer had been too simple.

“History has not been particularly generous to women whose buildings survived them,” Claire said. “Sometimes the building is the only witness they get.”

Adrian’s gaze returned to her.

“That sounds like something you’ve said before.”

“No. It sounds like something I’ve had reason to think before.”

She regretted the sentence immediately, not because it was untrue but because it had let too much biography into the room.

His expression changed by a degree.

He did not ask.

Another point.

Claire moved to the window instead.

From here the western lawn dropped towards a line of trees. Rain patterned the glass. The stone reveal was unusually deep—nearly half a metre, perhaps more. Thick walls were unsurprising in a country house, but Raven House had been built in the 1920s, not the 1620s.

She put two fingers against the plaster return, measuring by habit.

Behind her, Adrian said, “You’re doing it again.”

“What?”

“Looking at the house as if it has made a clerical error.”

“Buildings make them constantly.”

“Buildings?”

“Usually through the people who alter them.”

He came to stand a little way from the window. Not beside her. Close enough that she became aware of him and irritated enough with herself to resent the awareness.

Outside, the west wing projected beyond the line of the room.

Claire looked at the internal wall, then back through the glass.

“How old is this partition?”

“No idea.”

“Original?”

“I wouldn’t trust anyone in my family to know what original means.”

“That may be the first useful thing you’ve told me.”

“You make gratitude very attractive.”

She glanced at him.

He had said it without emphasis, but the sentence sat between professional banter and something else.

Claire chose the safer category.

“I’m not here to be attractive.”

“No,” Adrian said. “I gathered.”

That annoyed her more than flirtation would have.

She stepped away from the window.

“Show me the route through the west wing.”

His expression became innocent in a way she did not believe for a second.

“Now?”

“You have somewhere else to be?”

“Several places.”

“Any of them more expensive than hiring me to waste an afternoon?”

He picked up a ring of keys from the table.

“This way.”

The west corridor passed a morning room stripped of furniture, a narrow stair to the first floor and two locked bedrooms waiting for survey. Claire walked slowly, counting doorways, noting changes in floorboard width and skirting profile.

At the far end, Adrian unlocked a small linen room.

“That’s the last accessible room on this level.”

Claire stepped inside.

Shelving occupied one wall. The opposite wall was plain plaster. No window.

She stood still.

Adrian leaned against the doorframe.

“Orientation?”

“Be quiet.”

“Charming.”

She raised one finger.

The rain was audible somewhere beyond the wall. Not against a window. A softer sound, dispersed and faint.

Claire pictured the exterior elevation.

Three west-facing bays.

She pictured the corridor behind her, the morning room, the bedrooms, this linen store.

Something did not settle.

She went back into the corridor and walked to the end wall.

A framed twentieth-century landscape hung there: moorland, low sky, nothing of interest. Claire lifted it carefully from its hook and handed it to Adrian.

“What are you doing?”

“Looking.”

“For?”

“I’ll tell you when I know.”

Behind the picture the plaster had been repaired in a broad vertical strip. The texture differed from the surrounding wall. Newer paint, carefully matched. At skirting level, the oak had been interrupted and replaced with a shorter length whose grain ran the wrong way.

Claire crouched.

Adrian stopped joking.

She ran her fingertips above the skirting without touching the repair itself. A vertical line. Another on the opposite side, faint but present.

The proportions of a door.

Except there was no door.

She stood and looked along the corridor, then opened the folded survey plan from her pocket.

The modern plan showed a continuous wall.

No doorway.

No recess.

Nothing beyond it but the exterior line of the west wing.

Adrian came closer.

“What?”

Claire placed her finger on the plan, then pointed to the wall.

“According to this, that has never been an opening.”

“And?”

She looked at the repaired plaster.

“And someone bricked up a doorway your survey says did not exist.”

The rain moved through the walls with a sound too soft to be called a voice.

Claire kept looking at the plan.

For the first time since arriving, Raven House no longer seemed merely larger than it ought to be.

It seemed to be keeping its own measurements.
