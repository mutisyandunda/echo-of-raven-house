# Chapter 22: The Dead Woman’s Signature

## Chapter purpose
Harcourt produces the predecessor firm’s recovered 1932 conveyance; Claire spots Celeste’s purported signature dated after her death.

## Mystery movement
- **Clues:** C12
- **Red herrings:** R3 resolves
- Evidence must be observed before it is interpreted.
- POV inference cannot exceed the physical/documentary record.

## Romance / relationship movement
Adrian refuses the easy option of stopping the inquiry.

## End hook
The modern sale cannot proceed normally while the historic deed and first registration are under challenge.

## Draft

Peter Harcourt arrived carrying the past in a black document case and looking as though he blamed Claire for its weight.

“I would like it recorded,” he said before sitting, “that the predecessor packet arrived from storage this morning and that I have not previously had the original instrument in front of me.”

Claire opened her notebook.

“Recorded.”

“That was not an invitation to write it down.”

“It is now.”

Adrian closed the library door.

“Peter.”

Harcourt exhaled through his nose and removed a folder inside a transparent protective sleeve.

“The active title file contained the abstract we discussed. The predecessor archive contains the underlying 1932 conveyance.”

Claire did not reach for it.

“Original?”

“Original executed deed, so far as the custody history indicates. It was held by the predecessor firm, later boxed with dormant deeds when Raven House was first registered in 1991, and transferred into our off-site archive when the practices merged.”

“Continuous custody?”

“Documented from the predecessor storage ledger in 1951. Earlier custody is not clean.”

“That matters.”

“I am aware.”

Harcourt put the deed on the table.

The paper was heavier than the archive letters. Legal foolscap. Revenue markings. Wax residue where a seal had once been attached.

Claire read the date first.

12 March 1932.

Then the grantor.

**Celeste Vane.**

Her skin prickled.

Celeste had died on 17 October 1931.

Claire checked again because impossibility should always be forced to repeat itself.

March 1932.

Five months after death.

She looked at Harcourt.

“When did you notice?”

“Ten minutes after the packet reached my office.”

“And then?”

“I stopped substantive title work on the sale, notified the conveyancing team that a historical execution issue required specialist review, and drove here.”

Claire closed her notebook for a second.

“Good.”

Harcourt looked almost offended by the praise.

Adrian said, “What did the 1991 first-registration papers contain?”

“An abstract reciting this conveyance as the root by which Sebastian acquired the estate. The application did not reproduce the execution problem in a way that would have made Celeste’s date of death obvious. The modern register was then created in the Raven chain.”

“So if this is forged, the register is wrong.”

Harcourt held up a hand.

“Possibly. Do not simplify registered title into a family-tree exercise. A forged or otherwise invalid historic deed may raise questions about the basis on which first registration occurred, but modern alteration, rectification, possession, limitation and indemnity rules are their own field. Nobody in this room is going to declare a new owner tonight.”

Claire said, “Thank you.”

Adrian gave her a look.

“That sounded enthusiastic.”

“It was legally literate.”

Harcourt ignored them both.

Claire pointed to the execution block.

There was Celeste’s signature.

Or an attempt at it.

At a glance it resembled the signature on the certified marriage copy. Same sweeping C. Same narrow V.

At a second glance, it did not.

The terminal stroke of Vane was too short. The pressure pattern looked hesitant. Several letters had been drawn rather than written.

Claire photographed it beside a verified sample.

“Do not call this forged yet,” she said.

Harcourt nodded. “Agreed.”

“I was speaking to Adrian.”

“I hadn’t said anything.”

“You were about to.”

He gave her a look.

Claire continued.

“We have a deed dated after Celeste’s death bearing a signature purporting to be hers. Possibilities include a dating error, execution under an authority not apparent on the face of the deed, a mistaken death identity, or fraud.”

Harcourt said, “The first three are becoming difficult to sustain, but yes.”

Adrian leaned over the document.

“What does it actually do?”

“Purports to convey the Blackmere estate from Celeste to Sebastian for nominal consideration,” Harcourt said.

“Nominal?”

“Ten pounds and other consideration.”

Claire looked at the attestation clause.

“Witness?”

“A clerk associated with the predecessor firm. Dead since 1974.”

“Any power of attorney?”

“None found.”

“Executor signing?”

“No. The deed purports to be Celeste’s own execution.”

The room went quiet.

That was worse than a technical irregularity.

Harcourt continued.

“I have instructed a forensic document examiner and specialist property counsel. We will also obtain the complete 1991 first-registration file if available and identify exactly what was lodged.”

Adrian said, “So you did not know the title was defective.”

“I knew the modern title was registered and that its abstract began with a 1932 Vane-to-Raven conveyance. I did not know until today that the original deed purported to have been executed by a woman already dead.”

Claire watched Adrian absorb that.

Harcourt had not been guarding a murder secret.

He had been guarding process.

Annoyingly, those were not the same thing.

“Can the sale proceed?” Adrian asked.

Harcourt folded his hands.

“Not as though nothing has happened.”

“How unhelpful is that answer?”

“Very. It is also accurate. We can continue certain administrative preparations, but I will not advise exchange or give clean title replies while we are investigating a potentially forged root deed and the basis of first registration. If a credible adverse interest emerges, disclosure obligations become more serious.”

Adrian glanced at Claire.

“And if we stop the historical investigation?”

Harcourt paused.

The question settled heavily.

Claire said nothing.

She needed Adrian’s answer to belong to him.

Harcourt chose his words carefully.

“You cannot unknow the deed. Stopping Claire would not remove what is already in our possession. It might limit further historical facts, but it would not make the current problem disappear.”

“Good,” Claire said.

Adrian looked at her.

“Good?”

“It removes the coward’s shortcut.”

Harcourt’s expression suggested solicitors preferred cowardice to be called risk management.

Adrian looked down at the impossible signature.

“My father’s note says not to let discretion become dishonesty.”

“Marcus was not practising law when he wrote that,” Harcourt said.

“No. He was trying to leave me a conscience.”

Claire watched him.

This was the first real consequence.

Not an abstract possibility that the house had been stolen.

A sale that could stall.

Money that could disappear.

A registered title that might require litigation or alteration rather than a neat historical correction.

Adrian looked at Harcourt.

“We keep going.”

Peter closed his eyes briefly.

“Of course you do.”

“We preserve everything. We disclose what counsel says must be disclosed. Claire gets independent advice if the Mercer connection becomes a personal claim. And nobody describes the house as unquestionably ours until we understand what happened.”

Claire felt something inside her ease.

Not because the choice benefited her.

Because he made it before asking what it would cost.

Harcourt gathered his papers except the deed, which remained for supervised examination.

“The examiner will need the original under controlled conditions,” he said. “If forgery becomes the working conclusion, we will deal with the modern registration consequences through counsel and, if necessary, HM Land Registry. There may be rectification issues. There may be indemnity issues. There may be no simple route to either.”

“Understood,” Adrian said.

At the door Harcourt paused.

“For what it is worth, Dr Mercer, I did not know Celeste was Sebastian’s wife.”

“I believe you.”

“You say that as though it disappoints you.”

“It makes the story less convenient.”

“Reality often does.”

He left.

Adrian remained by the table.

Claire looked at the two signatures side by side.

Marriage record, 1924.

Conveyance, 1932.

One made while Celeste was alive.

One apparently made after she was dead.

“Thank you,” she said.

“For what?”

“Not stopping.”

He looked at her.

“I nearly wanted there to be a legal trick that made the question disappear.”

“That is different from stopping.”

“Barely.”

“No. Wanting an easy answer and choosing one are different things.”

He held her gaze.

The distinction seemed to matter to him.

“When Peter said the modern register complicated everything,” Adrian said, “part of me was relieved.”

“Of course.”

“Then I thought about a woman apparently signing away her house five months after she was buried.”

Claire touched the edge of the document sleeve.

“That tends to focus the mind.”

His smile was brief.

The forensic examiner would decide whether the signature was copied, simulated or something stranger.

For now they had something simpler and more disturbing.

A deed said a dead woman had conveyed Raven House.

And because the modern title had later been built on that documentary chain, the sale had stopped being merely a transaction.

It had become a problem no competent buyer’s solicitor could responsibly ignore.
