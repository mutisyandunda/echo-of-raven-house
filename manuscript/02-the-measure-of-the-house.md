# Chapter 2: The Measure of the House

## Chapter purpose

## Draft

Claire measured the corridor three times because she disliked being impressed by a mistake.

The first measurement came to eleven metres, eighty-four centimetres from the turn beside the morning room to the repaired plaster at the end wall.

The second came to eleven metres, eighty-three.

The third came to eleven metres, eighty-four again.

She wrote the figure in her notebook and looked at Adrian.

He was holding the far end of the laser tape against the skirting, one shoulder braced beneath the landscape painting she had removed the previous afternoon.

“Well?” he said.

“You’re too pleased.”

“I’m standing in a corridor holding a measuring device before breakfast. I promise you pleasure is not the dominant emotion.”

“You think I’m going to prove myself wrong.”

“I think old survey plans are often wrong.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It sounded kinder.”

Claire lowered the laser and checked the exterior figure again.

She had gone outside at seven-thirty, before the rain became serious, and measured the west elevation with a telescopic rod and the surveyor’s laser Adrian had produced from a cupboard with the quiet triumph of a man who enjoyed owning the correct tool. From the western corner of the central block to the end of the wing, the external distance—adjusted for the stone return—should have yielded an internal run roughly seventy centimetres longer than the one beneath her feet.

Allowing for wall thickness did not solve it.

Allowing for the chimney mass did not solve it.

Allowing for the linen cupboard did not solve it.

The house contained space that the rooms did not.

“Again,” she said.

Adrian shut his eyes briefly.

“You know repetition does not make geometry more obedient.”

“No, but it does make testimony stronger.”

“Are we litigating against the corridor?”

“Potentially.”

He moved the laser to the opposite skirting.

Claire reset the device.

The red dot settled near his shoe.

Eleven metres, eighty-four.

She wrote it down.

Adrian looked at the notebook. “Do I get to know what the house has been convicted of?”

“Not yet.”

“You have been saying that for twelve hours.”

“And yet you continue to ask premature questions.”

“You say premature as if curiosity is a moral weakness.”

“In archives, it often is.”

He smiled despite himself.

Claire did not.

The smile was becoming inconvenient.

She walked back through the west rooms and measured the principal internal walls individually: morning room, bedroom, service stair enclosure, second bedroom, linen room. Adrian followed, holding doors, moving a chair when asked and making only two comments about being demoted to equipment assistant.

The sum was still wrong.

Seventy-eight centimetres.

Not much in a building of this scale. Too much to vanish accidentally.

Claire returned to the end wall.

“The void is somewhere along this line.”

Adrian leaned against the frame of the linen-room door. “Void.”

“Unaccounted-for volume.”

“That sounds less ominous.”

“It isn’t ominous.”

“You say that while staring at a wall that apparently contains nearly a metre of missing house.”

“Buildings are full of voids. Service runs. flues. ventilation. structural pockets. Bad alterations.”

“Secret rooms?”

“No.”

“You sound disappointed.”

“I sound employed.”

That earned a short laugh.

It did something strange to the corridor. The walls returned the sound a fraction later—not as an obvious echo, but as a pale imitation from somewhere to Claire’s left.

She turned.

“What?” Adrian asked.

“Do that again.”

“No.”

“Laugh.”

“I have professional standards.”

“You were laughing at me thirty seconds ago.”

“That was spontaneous.”

“Fine. Say something.”

“I have been doing that all morning.”

“Louder.”

He straightened. “Claire.”

The name went down the corridor cleanly.

Nothing.

She frowned.

“Compelling experiment.”

“Again.”

“Claire.”

Nothing.

He looked pleased.

Then, faintly, from the wall beside the linen room, came the smallest returning shape of the final consonant.

Not Claire.

Not a word.

Just the remnant of one.

Claire moved closer.

“Say something longer.”

Adrian considered. “I deeply regret agreeing to this.”

The corridor took his sentence and broke it apart.

Most of it died at once. But a fragment—*agreeing to this*—seemed to travel sideways through the plaster and return from farther west.

Adrian stopped smiling.

Claire pressed the heel of her hand against the wall.

Solid enough.

“Ventilation duct?” he said.

“Possibly.”

“Speaking tube?”

“Possibly.”

“Ghost?”

“No.”

“That one sounded very certain.”

“I have standards too.”

They spent the next hour testing the west wing like two people trying to embarrass a building into confession.

Claire stood in the morning room and spoke towards the chimney breast while Adrian waited in the corridor. Then they swapped positions. They opened doors, shut doors, moved curtains, checked vents, peered behind radiator covers added decades after the house was built. The effect came and went. Certain sounds travelled. Others disappeared. Low voices behaved differently from sharp ones.

At one point Adrian said from the other side of the wall, “Can you hear me?”

Claire heard nothing.

Then, from behind her, near a bookcase on the opposite wall, his voice arrived softly enough to feel intimate.

*Hear me?*

She turned so quickly she struck her hip on a table.

Adrian came through the door a second later.

“You did hear that.”

“Yes.”

“And it came from there.”

“Yes.”

“That is deeply unpleasant.”

“It is acoustically interesting.”

“Those are not mutually exclusive.”

Claire ignored the pain in her hip and went to the bookcase.

The shelves were built into a shallow recess. She removed three books, all late-twentieth-century gardening titles, and examined the backing board.

A line of small drilled holes ran near the top, almost hidden by shadow.

Not modern shelf fittings.

Air holes, perhaps.

Or acoustic openings.

She photographed them.

Adrian crouched beside her. “Original?”

“The timber could be. The holes look intentional.”

“How can you tell?”

“Spacing. Same diameter. Clean alignment.”

“You enjoy when things line up.”

“I enjoy when they tell the truth.”

He looked at her for half a second too long.

Claire stood.

“Archive.”

“What?”

“If this is original, Celeste may have drawn it.”

They returned to the temporary workroom and opened the first box labelled VANE / PLANS.

The folders had been sleeved but not fully catalogued. Claire worked slowly, calling out references while Adrian entered them into the provisional spreadsheet.

“Ground floor presentation plan,” she said. “Unsigned copy. Probably later.”

“Later than what?”

“Than the design phase. Too clean.”

The next sheet was tracing paper, brittle at the folds, covered in pencil amendments and red ink.

Claire placed weights at the corners.

There it was: the west wing, drawn with far more internal complexity than the modern survey showed.

Not a hidden room. At least not clearly.

A narrow service chase ran behind the morning room wall, interrupted by what looked like ventilation shafts and maintenance access points.

Claire leaned closer.

At four positions, Celeste had drawn tiny paired curves in the margin.

Two crescents facing each other.

Adrian pointed. “What’s that?”

“I don’t know.”

“Decorative flourish?”

“On a working plan?”

“You tell me.”

Claire checked the sheet legend.

No symbol matched.

She turned to another drawing.

The same mark appeared beside the library.

A third drawing placed it near the west stair.

A fourth beside a service passage.

Always in pairs.

Always at points where two spaces nearly touched without an obvious doorway.

Claire photographed each one.

“Could be notation for sound,” Adrian said.

“Why?”

“They look like little echoes.”

She glanced at him.

“That is not a technical answer.”

“You asked what I thought.”

“I did not.”

“You were about to.”

“I absolutely was not.”

He leaned back in the chair, satisfied.

The morning had changed something between them. Not much. Claire would not have called it ease. Ease was too generous and too dangerous.

Competence recognised competence; that was all.

She returned to the plan.

In the lower right corner, beneath Celeste’s initials, a note had been erased so thoroughly that only the pressure of the pencil remained.

Claire tilted the sheet under the lamp.

Adrian stopped typing.

“You see something.”

“Maybe.”

She took a raking-light photograph, then another from the side.

Three words, perhaps. The first illegible. The last might have been *line*.

She would not guess.

“What now?” he asked.

“Now I do not decide what I want it to say.”

“That seems inconvenient for mystery novels.”

“This is not a mystery novel.”

He looked towards the corridor.

“You’re sure?”

Claire almost smiled.

Instead she opened the next folder.

By late afternoon she had enough to state the problem cleanly.

The modern west-wing survey omitted approximately seventy-eight centimetres of cumulative depth along part of the internal run. Celeste’s original drawings showed narrow service and ventilation structures in roughly the same zone. Several drawings carried an unexplained paired-curve mark at architectural junctions where sound had behaved strangely during their tests.

Nothing supernatural.

Nothing, yet, criminal.

Just a house with more interior structure than its current plan admitted.

Claire printed a copy of the modern survey and marked the discrepancy in red.

Adrian stood behind her shoulder.

“Seventy-eight centimetres.”

“Approximately.”

“Enough to crawl through?”

“For someone narrow and determined.”

“Neither of us, then.”

Claire looked at him.

“You are very confident about my level of determination.”

“Not remotely.”

She capped the pen.

“Tomorrow Jonah can examine the wall fabric. No opening until he does.”

“You are learning to respect my house.”

“I respect buildings.”

“That was not what I said.”

“No.”

The room became quiet.

Rain had started again, lighter now, tapping at the high windows.

Claire gathered the drawings into their sleeves.

Adrian had already reached the doorway when she heard a woman speak.

Not loudly.

Not clearly.

Just beyond the wall to her right.

A low voice, conversational, as if someone stood in the next room speaking to another person.

Claire froze.

Adrian turned.

“What?”

She lifted a hand.

The voice came again.

Three or four syllables. Too blurred to understand.

Claire crossed to the wall.

“There’s someone in there.”

Adrian’s face changed.

“There is no room there.”

The voice travelled once more through the plaster, softened by distance and structure.

Then silence.

Claire looked at the red-marked survey on the table.

Seventy-eight centimetres of missing house.

And, somewhere inside it, a voice.
